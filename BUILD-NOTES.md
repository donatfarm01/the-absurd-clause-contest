Build notes for The Absurd Clause Contest entry.

The bounty asks for one original English sentence of at most 200 characters, phrased as an absurd clause that could sit buried deep inside a terms-of-service agreement. A judge model compares the clauses in pairs and the funniest one wins. The judge's brief weighs, most important first: whether the line is an absurd clause that could hide in a terms of service, whether it is funny on its own with no outside setup, whether it sounds like real legal boilerplate until the absurdity hits, whether it is original and specific with a concrete image, and whether it is tight with no wasted words.

Fase one of the creative playbook looked at the judge persona, a deadpan comedy editor who has read more terms of service than anyone alive, and at the cliche radar. The lines ninety percent of entrants will write are the obvious ones: selling your soul, selling your data, terms that may change at any time, robot jokes, and agreeing to be happy. All of those paths were ruled out.

Fase two drafted three contrasting concepts. Concept A, the sharp utility angle, settled disputes by a single-elimination arm-wrestling tournament on the second Tuesday of March, loser pays for lunch, at 166 characters. Concept B, the contrarian angle, was a recursive consent clause where reading the clause counts as agreeing that you read the clause, at 121 characters. Concept C, the high-craft fusion, opened like a genuine account-succession provision and named the heir as the user's eldest houseplant, subject to a ninety day probationary period, at 130 characters.

Fase three scored them on hook, originality, sponsor fit, and clarity, each out of ten. Concept A scored 8, 6, 9, 9 for a total of 32, losing points because dispute by combat is a known trope. Concept B scored 7, 5, 8, 9 for a total of 29, losing points because recursive consent is well worn and only works once the reader already knows it is a joke. Concept C scored 9, 9, 10, 10 for a total of 38 and won: it opens exactly like real succession boilerplate, the eldest houseplant ambushes the reader, the probationary period is a second quiet joke, and the image is specific rather than a vague shrug at the genre.

The winning entry is the Concept C line, one sentence of 130 characters. The program is a small Python script that writes that fixed line to the output entry file, asserting the length stays within the one to two hundred character gate and that the line carries no leading or trailing whitespace. The script was run fresh twice and produced byte-identical output both times, and the hashes of the entry as written and stripped of whitespace were computed and match, since the file carries no trailing newline.

The repository holds the entry program, a readme with the three live make dot fun badges in the slug-prefixed form from the kit template, an MIT license, and the owner file holding the primary payout wallet. The kit check was run from the unpacked kit directory and passed its four local checks, with the transcripts check failing exactly as expected because the parent supplies the transcripts at bundle time.
